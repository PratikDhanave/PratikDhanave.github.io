Why Generating Images Is Hard — The Problem Diffusion Solved

Every text-to-image system — Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, DALL·E, Imagen — rests on one idea: destroy an image with noise, then learn to run the destruction backwards. Before that idea, a decade of generative modeling fought a three-way trade-off between sample quality, training stability, and diversity — and every architecture picked two. This series builds diffusion models from first principles: what problem they solve, why the math works, and how the pieces (latents, guidance, transformers, flow matching) assembled into the systems generating images and video today.

Why generation is sampling (not prediction), how VAEs blur and GANs collapse, the quality/diversity/stability trilemma — and the iterative-refinement move that escaped it, opening a 10-post series.

The Forward Process — Destroying Images with Noise, Carefully

Diffusion models begin with the only part of the pipeline that involves no learning at all: a fixed, mechanical procedure that gradually turns any image into pure static. It seems almost too simple to matter — yet every design choice in it (Gaussian noise, small steps, a schedule, a closed-form shortcut) is load-bearing for everything the model later learns. Understanding the destruction is half of understanding the generation.

The forward process is a fixed Markov chain that turns any image into pure Gaussian static — no learning anywhere. Why Gaussian noise (closure), what the noise schedule allocates (practice time across corruption levels), the closed-form jump that makes training random-access, and the known endpoint generation starts from.

Learning to Denoise — DDPM and the Loss That Trains Every Image Model

The reverse of destruction is generation — but only if a network can learn to run one small step of the destruction backwards. The surprise at the heart of DDPM is how plain the training recipe turned out to be: corrupt an image, ask the network which noise was added, score it with mean squared error. No adversary, no instability — a regression loss so simple it reads like a tutorial exercise, hiding a derivation that connects it to maximum likelihood.

Small Gaussian forward steps make each reverse step approximately Gaussian — so generation reduces to regression. DDPM's recipe: predict the added noise, score it with plain mean squared error, sample by walking the chain backwards with fresh noise injected each step. Four lines of training loop, and why they beat the trilemma.

The Score-Based View — Gradients, SDEs, and Why Denoising Learns Direction

DDPM looks like a clever trick: predict noise, subtract it, repeat. The score-based view reveals it as something deeper — the denoiser is secretly learning the gradient of the data distribution, the arrow that points from anywhere in image space toward "more like a real image." Two research threads, denoising and score matching, turned out to be the same thing seen from different angles, and their unification under stochastic differential equations is what every modern sampler and flow-matching method builds on.

The denoiser is secretly learning the score — the gradient of log-probability pointing toward more-plausible images. Langevin dynamics, why denoising and score matching are the same computation, and the continuous limit: a reverse-time SDE and its deterministic twin, the probability flow ODE, where fast sampling and flow matching live.

Sampling Faster — DDIM and the Art of Skipping Steps

A thousand network evaluations per image is not a product. The first great act of cost reduction in diffusion was realizing the thousand-step walk could be shortened without retraining anything: the same network, asked smarter questions, gets there in fifty steps — or twenty. This post covers DDIM and the sampler zoo it opened, the step-count economics that made diffusion deployable, and why samplers are the rare component you can swap after training.

DDPM's thousand steps were a property of one sampler, not the trained network. DDIM's deterministic walk follows the probability flow ODE — 50 steps, zero retraining, plus invertible noise-space addresses — and the sampler zoo is just numerical analysis: a ~40× cost cut from math alone, down to the ~10-step curvature floor.

Latent Diffusion — Why Stable Diffusion Doesn't Diffuse Pixels

The single decision most responsible for putting image generation on consumer GPUs was not a better denoiser or a faster sampler — it was moving the entire diffusion process out of pixel space. Latent diffusion compresses images roughly 48× with a learned autoencoder, runs every denoising step in that compressed space, and decodes once at the end. Same theory as posts 2–5, radically different economics: this is the architecture decision that made Stable Diffusion stable to run.

Stable Diffusion doesn't diffuse pixels. A perceptually-trained autoencoder compresses images ~48×, the entire diffusion apparatus runs unchanged on latents, and a decoder re-synthesizes texture once at the end. The economics, the three-piece architecture, the autoencoder-as-quality-ceiling caveat, and why video inherited the pattern.

Conditioning and Classifier-Free Guidance — How Text Steers the Image

Everything so far generates an image — any image the training distribution contains. A product needs the image you asked for. Conditioning is how a prompt gets wired into every denoising step, and classifier-free guidance — a two-line trick discovered almost apologetically — is the single knob that most determines whether the output actually obeys the prompt. The famous "CFG scale" slider in every image UI is this post.

Conditioning wires the prompt into every denoising step via text encoders and cross-attention — but the honest conditional distribution follows prompts too loosely. Classifier-free guidance fixes it: co-train an unconditional mode, then extrapolate the difference at sampling. One knob for fidelity vs diversity, 2× cost, and negative prompts for free.

From U-Net to Diffusion Transformer — The Architecture Underneath the Denoiser

For the first years of diffusion, "the model" meant a U-Net — a convolutional hourglass borrowed from medical image segmentation. Then the same migration that had already run through NLP and vision ran through generation: replace the handcrafted spatial architecture with a plain transformer over patch tokens, and let scale do the work. The Diffusion Transformer (DiT) is now the backbone of the frontier image and video systems — and the reasons why are a case study in what architectures are actually for.

The denoiser was a U-Net — convolutional hourglass, skip connections, interleaved cross-attention — until the Diffusion Transformer re-answered the same requirements generically: patchify the latent, run a standard transformer, condition via adaptive layer norm. The real win is economic: LLM-style scaling curves, inherited infrastructure, and spacetime patches that set up video.

Flow Matching — Straightening the Path from Noise to Image

Diffusion arrived at "follow a velocity field from noise to data" the hard way — through Markov chains, variational bounds, and reverse-time SDEs. Flow matching asks the question that framing begs: if generation is just transporting noise to data along a path, why inherit a curved path from a noising process you never actually cared about? Why not simply choose the path — and choose it straight? The answer now trains the frontier: Stable Diffusion 3, Flux, and much of the current video generation stack are flow-matching models.

If generation is transport from noise to data, why inherit a curved path from a noising process? Flow matching chooses the path — straight — and regresses a network onto its constant velocity: image minus noise. Rectified flow straightens further toward one-step sampling, and CFG, DiT, and latents all carry over unchanged.

One Step and Sixty Frames — Distillation, Consistency Models, and Video Diffusion

The series opened with a trade: diffusion bought quality and stability by paying in steps. Every post since has haggled the price down — better solvers, smaller spaces, straighter paths. This closing post covers the two endgames: collapsing the walk to a single stride (distillation and consistency models), and spending the budget on the hardest canvas there is — video, where the pixel count explodes but the architecture barely changes. It ends with the mental model the whole series compresses into.

The step bill falls to one by learning destinations instead of following paths: progressive distillation halves step counts, adversarial losses restore sharpness, consistency models make point-to-endpoint the objective itself. Then video — the same stack over spacetime patches, where temporal coherence is just attention across time. Closes the series with the factored mental model.
