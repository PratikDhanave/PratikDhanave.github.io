Why Graphs Need Their Own Deep Learning

Neural networks conquered images, text, and audio — but a huge amount of the world's most valuable data isn't any of those. Graph neural networks are the architecture built for data whose meaning lives in its connections, and this series builds them from first principles.

Node Embeddings and Graph Learning Tasks

Before diving into how graph neural networks work, it's worth being precise about what they produce and what problems they solve. The output is embeddings — vectors that place each node (or edge, or whole graph) in a meaningful space — and the problems come in three flavors that cover almost every graph application. Getting this vocabulary straight makes the architectures in the rest of the series click into place.

A GNN produces embeddings that fuse what a node is (features) with where it sits (structure), and graph problems come in three levels: node-level (classification), edge-level (link prediction — recommendation, knowledge-graph completion), and graph-level (molecule classification). Message Passing — The Core Mechanism of Every GNN

Strip away the variations and every graph neural network does the same fundamental thing: each node repeatedly gathers information from its neighbors and updates itself. This is message passing, and it's the single idea you need to understand GNNs — GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT, and the rest are all just different choices for how a node listens to its neighbors. This post builds message passing from scratch and shows why it elegantly satisfies everything a graph architecture must.

Graph Convolutional Networks — The First Great GNN

The graph convolutional network is the "linear regression" of graph deep learning: the simplest architecture worth knowing, the one that made GNNs practical, and the baseline every other method is compared against. It's message passing with the most natural possible aggregation — average your neighbors — and understanding exactly what it does, and where its simplicity bites, is the foundation for everything more sophisticated.

The GCN is the 'logistic regression' of graph deep learning — message passing with the simplest aggregation (degree-normalized averaging), the strong baseline every method is compared against. GraphSAGE — Inductive Learning and Scaling to Big Graphs

The graph convolutional network has two problems that make it impractical for real systems: it can't handle new nodes, and it needs the whole graph in memory at once. GraphSAGE fixes both with one shift in perspective — learn a reusable function that builds a node's embedding from a sample of its neighbors, rather than learning a fixed embedding per node. This is the idea that made GNNs usable on billion-node industrial graphs, and it's why most production GNNs are GraphSAGE-style.

The GCN can't handle new nodes and needs the whole graph in memory. GraphSAGE fixes both: learn a reusable function that builds an embedding from a sample of neighbors, rather than a fixed per-node vector. Inductive learning and neighbor sampling — the ideas that made GNNs work on billion-node industrial graphs.

Graph Attention Networks — Learning Which Neighbors Matter

The graph convolutional network treats all of a node's neighbors as (near) equals. But that's often wrong — some neighbors are far more relevant than others, and which ones depends on the context. Graph attention networks fix this by letting the model learn how much to weigh each neighbor, bringing the attention mechanism that revolutionized language models to graphs. This post explains attention on graphs and why it's so often the right upgrade over plain averaging.

The GCN treats all neighbors as equal, but some matter far more and which ones is context-dependent. Graph attention networks learn data-dependent weights so a node listens more to important neighbors — the transformer's attention mechanism adapted to graphs — with interpretability as a bonus. Training and Scaling GNNs — The Practical Realities

Graph neural networks look clean on paper and get messy in production, because training on graphs breaks assumptions that ordinary deep learning takes for granted. The data points aren't independent, the graph may not fit in memory, and the model fails in graph-specific ways. This post covers the practical realities of making GNNs actually work at scale — the problems you'll hit and the techniques that address them.

GNNs look clean on paper and get messy in production because the data points aren't independent.
