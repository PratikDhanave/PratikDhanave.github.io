# Product Marketing

*There's a role that sits exactly on the seam between engineering and the market — translating what was built into what customers understand and want — and it's the one technical companies most often do badly or not at all. Product marketing is that bridge. When it's missing, you get the classic technical-company failure: a genuinely great product described in terms only its builders understand, launched into silence, losing to a worse product that explained itself better. Product marketing is how good engineering becomes a product the market actually gets.*

Product marketing is the discipline that connects the *product* to the *market* — turning what you built into value the market understands, wants, and buys. This post covers what product marketing is, its core jobs (positioning/messaging, launches, sales enablement, competitive intelligence), and why it's especially critical — and especially neglected — at technical companies. It's the marketing discipline closest to engineering, and the one that most directly determines whether good products succeed.

## What product marketing is

**Product marketing** is the bridge between the product and the market: it's responsible for understanding the market and customers, defining how the product is *positioned and communicated*, and ensuring the product reaches and resonates with the right customers. It sits between engineering/product (who build) and sales/marketing/customers (who sell and buy), translating in both directions:

- **It translates product into value.** Product marketing takes what the product *is and does* (features, capabilities — the builder's view) and translates it into what it *means for customers* (value, outcomes, why it matters — the customer's view). This translation — from features to value, from technical capability to customer benefit — is the core job, and it's exactly what technical builders struggle with (the "engineer's positioning trap" from the GTM series). Product marketing is the discipline dedicated to bridging that gap.
- **It brings the market back to the product.** The bridge runs both ways: product marketing also carries *market and customer understanding* back to the product/engineering side — what customers need, how they talk, what competitors do — informing what gets built and how it's framed. It keeps the product connected to market reality.
- **It's the most product-adjacent marketing.** Of the marketing disciplines, product marketing is closest to the product and to engineering — it requires understanding the product deeply *and* the market deeply. This makes it a natural bridge role and, for technical people, often the most relatable marketing discipline (it's about deeply understanding a technical product and communicating it accurately).

Product marketing, then, is the connective tissue between what you build and the market that might want it — translating product into customer value and market understanding back into product. It's the discipline most directly responsible for ensuring a good product is *understood* and *wanted*, which is why its absence is so damaging to technical companies. Its work breaks into a few core jobs.

## Positioning and messaging

The foundational job of product marketing is **positioning and messaging** (covered strategically in the GTM series — here as product marketing's core deliverable):

- **Positioning: what the product is, for whom, and why it's the right choice.** Product marketing owns defining the product's positioning — its place in the market relative to alternatives, its target customer, and its key differentiated value. This is the strategic foundation everything else communicates (the GTM series covers positioning in depth). Product marketing makes this concrete for the specific product.
- **Messaging: how the value is expressed.** From positioning, product marketing develops the *messaging* — the clear articulation of the product's value in the customer's language, leading with benefits/outcomes rather than features (the anti-"feature-first" discipline from the GTM series). This messaging is what the website, sales team, ads, and product itself all use — so product marketing effectively authors how the product is *talked about* everywhere.
- **Consistency across touchpoints.** A key product-marketing job is ensuring the message is *consistent* — the same clear value story wherever a customer encounters the product. Product marketing is the source of truth for "how we talk about this product," keeping messaging coherent across all the places and people that communicate it.

Positioning and messaging are product marketing's central deliverable: a clear, consistent, value-focused story of what the product is and why it matters, translated from the product's capabilities into the customer's terms. This is precisely the translation technical teams struggle to do themselves — which is why dedicated product marketing (or at least the discipline, done deliberately) is so valuable for technical products. Getting the message right is most of the battle.

## Launches, enablement, and competitive intelligence

Beyond positioning/messaging, product marketing has several other core jobs that carry the product to market:

- **Product launches.** Product marketing plans and executes *launches* — bringing new products or features to market: the messaging, the announcement, coordinating the channels, and driving awareness and adoption. (As the GTM series noted, a launch is a start, not a finish — good product marketing treats launch as the beginning of driving adoption, not a one-day event.) Launches are a visible, high-stakes product-marketing responsibility: getting a new thing understood and adopted.
- **Sales enablement.** For products with a sales motion, product marketing *enables sales* — equipping the sales team with the messaging, materials, competitive information, and understanding they need to sell effectively. Product marketing ensures salespeople can clearly communicate the product's value and handle objections. It's the bridge between "how we position the product" and "how sales conveys it" (connecting to the sales/presales series).
- **Competitive intelligence.** Product marketing understands the *competitive landscape* — who the alternatives are, their strengths/weaknesses, and how to position against them — and equips the company (sales, product, marketing) to compete. Knowing how you compare and how to articulate your differentiation is a product-marketing job, feeding both messaging and sales.
- **Market and customer insight.** Underlying all of it, product marketing develops deep understanding of customers and the market (through research, talking to customers, analyzing the market) — which informs positioning, messaging, launches, and what the product should be. This insight is the foundation the other jobs rest on.

These jobs — launches, sales enablement, competitive intelligence, and market insight — are how product marketing carries the product into the market and equips the company to win. Together with positioning/messaging, they make product marketing the discipline that ensures a product is well-understood, well-launched, well-sold, and well-differentiated. Each is a place technical companies often fall short by neglecting the discipline.

## Why it's critical (and neglected) at technical companies

Product marketing is *especially* important and *especially* underinvested at technical companies — the combination that causes so many good technical products to underperform:

- **Technical companies build well but communicate poorly.** Engineering-driven companies are often excellent at *building* and poor at *translating* what they built into customer value (the engineer's feature-first tendency). Product marketing is exactly the discipline that fixes this — but technical companies frequently *don't invest in it* (seeing it as unnecessary "marketing"), so the translation gap goes unfilled. The result: great products described in builder's terms, poorly understood by the market.
- **The classic failure mode.** Without product marketing, you get the recurring technical-company failure: a superior product that *loses* because it was poorly positioned, unclearly messaged, badly launched, and weakly differentiated — out-competed by an inferior product that communicated its value better. This is the GTM series' "product isn't enough" lesson, and product marketing is the specific discipline that addresses it.
- **You need the discipline even without the title.** Not every company has a "product marketer," but every company needs the *work* done — someone must translate the product into clear value, position it, message it, launch it well, and enable its selling. At technical companies, founders and engineers often must do this themselves (or hire for it), and doing it deliberately — rather than neglecting it — is what lets good products succeed. Recognizing product marketing as essential work, not optional fluff, is the key shift.

Product marketing is the bridge between product and market — translating capabilities into customer value through positioning and messaging, and carrying the product to market via launches, sales enablement, and competitive intelligence. It's the marketing discipline closest to engineering and the most critical (and most neglected) at technical companies, where it directly addresses the "great product, poor communication" failure. Next: content marketing and SEO — a powerful, honest way to attract technical audiences.

## Key takeaways

- Product marketing is the bridge between the product and the market: it translates what the product *is and does* (the builder's feature view) into what it *means for customers* (value, outcomes) and carries market/customer understanding back to the product — the connective tissue ensuring a good product is understood and wanted.
- Its central deliverable is positioning and messaging — defining what the product is, for whom, and why it's the right choice, then expressing that value clearly in the customer's language (benefits over features) and consistently across every touchpoint (product marketing is the source of truth for "how we talk about this product").
- Its other core jobs are product launches (bringing new products/features to market — a start, not a one-day finish), sales enablement (equipping sales with messaging/materials/competitive info), competitive intelligence (knowing the landscape and how to position against it), and underlying market/customer insight.
- It's the marketing discipline closest to engineering (requiring deep product *and* market understanding), which makes it the most relatable to technical people and the natural bridge role — and exactly the discipline that fixes the engineer's "feature-first, poorly-translated" tendency.
- It's critical *and* neglected at technical companies — which build well but communicate poorly and often dismiss product marketing as unnecessary — causing the classic failure of superior products losing to better-communicated inferior ones; the key shift is recognizing product marketing as essential work (done deliberately, even without the title), not optional fluff.
