# Natural Experiments — When the World Randomizes for You

*Sometimes you can't run an experiment, but the world runs one for you. A policy change, a lottery, an arbitrary cutoff, a quirk of timing — these can assign a "treatment" in a way that's effectively random, letting you recover experimental-grade causal evidence from observational data. Natural experiments are the most ingenious tools in causal inference, and the quasi-experimental methods built on them (instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity) are how economists and data scientists establish causation when a controlled trial is impossible.*

Post 6 covered adjustment methods and their unprovable "no unmeasured confounding" assumption. This post covers a cleverer escape: find a source of *as-if-random* variation in the real world, so you get randomization's credibility (post 4) without running the experiment — sidestepping confounding rather than adjusting for it. These are the techniques behind much of modern empirical economics and increasingly data science.

## The idea: find as-if-random variation

The core move is to find something that assigned the treatment **in a way unrelated to the confounders** — not because you randomized, but because *circumstance did*. If some external quirk nudged people into treatment for reasons that have nothing to do with the outcome's other causes, then comparing those nudged in vs out is like comparing randomized groups. You're borrowing randomization from the world instead of creating it.

The reason this is so valuable: it attacks the exact weakness of observational methods (post 6). Adjustment *assumes* you've captured all confounders; a natural experiment instead finds variation that *couldn't* be confounded (because the quirk was arbitrary), giving the confounding-proof credibility of a real experiment. When a credible natural experiment exists, it's the strongest causal evidence available short of running an RCT yourself — which is why researchers prize them and hunt for them. The three canonical designs each exploit a different kind of as-if-random variation.

## The three canonical designs

- **Instrumental variables (IV).** Find an **instrument** — a variable that affects the *treatment* but influences the *outcome only through the treatment*, and is itself unrelated to the confounders. The instrument provides a slice of treatment variation that's as-if-random, and you use *only that slice* to estimate the effect, bypassing confounding. Classic example: to estimate the effect of military service on earnings, use the **draft lottery** as an instrument — the lottery number randomly nudged people into service but has no other path to earnings, so the lottery-driven variation in service is clean. IV is powerful but rests on the instrument's validity, which is often arguable and hard to verify: the instrument must *only* affect the outcome through the treatment (the "exclusion restriction"), an assumption you largely have to defend rather than prove.
- **Difference-in-differences (DiD).** When a treatment hits one group but not another at a known time, compare the *change* in outcomes for the treated group to the *change* for the untreated group. By looking at *differences in differences*, you subtract out both fixed differences between the groups *and* general time trends, isolating the treatment's effect. Classic for policy evaluation: a law changes in one state but not a neighbor, and you compare how each state's outcome changed. Its key assumption is **parallel trends** — that absent the treatment, the two groups would have moved in parallel — which you can partly check pre-treatment but must assume for the post period.
- **Regression discontinuity (RDD).** Exploit an arbitrary **cutoff** that assigns treatment. People just *above* and just *below* a threshold are essentially identical (it's arbitrary which side of the line they fell), but one group gets treated and the other doesn't — a local randomization at the boundary. Compare outcomes right around the cutoff. Example: a scholarship awarded to students scoring above an exact test threshold — those at 89 and 90 are comparable, so the outcome jump at the cutoff estimates the scholarship's effect. RDD is credible precisely at the boundary, but its estimate is *local* (only valid near the cutoff) and needs a genuinely arbitrary threshold.

Each design is a different way of answering "where's the as-if-random variation?" — an instrument, a policy-and-time contrast, or an arbitrary cutoff — and each turns a quirk of the world into a causal lever.

## The power, the assumptions, and the honest limits

Natural experiments are ingenious, but they trade one kind of assumption for another, and using them well means being clear-eyed about that:

- **They replace "no unmeasured confounding" with design-specific assumptions** that are often *more plausible and more defensible* — but still assumptions, not guarantees. IV needs a valid instrument (exclusion restriction); DiD needs parallel trends; RDD needs a truly arbitrary cutoff and no manipulation around it. Each can fail, and much of the craft is arguing (and stress-testing) that the specific assumption holds.
- **They're opportunistic.** You can't conjure a natural experiment on demand — you need the world to have provided one (a lottery, a policy discontinuity, a threshold). So they're available for *some* questions and not others, and a big part of empirical skill is *recognizing* when a credible natural experiment exists in your setting.
- **Estimates are often local.** RDD estimates the effect *near the cutoff*; IV estimates the effect *for those moved by the instrument* (the "compliers") — not necessarily the average effect for everyone. The credible number may not be the number you most wanted (external-validity limits, like post 4).
- **They demand scrutiny.** Because the whole thing rests on the as-if-random claim, natural experiments live or die on whether that claim is believable, and good practice subjects it to hard tests (checking pre-trends for DiD, testing instrument plausibility, looking for manipulation around RDD cutoffs).

Used well, natural experiments are a genuine middle path between the gold-standard-but-often-impossible RCT (post 4) and the always-available-but-assumption-heavy adjustment methods (post 6): they bring experiment-like credibility to observational settings by finding randomization the world already did. For engineers and analysts, the practical takeaway is to *look* for them — when a feature rolled out to one region first, when a policy changed at a date, when access was gated by an arbitrary threshold, you may have a natural experiment hiding in your data that gives a far stronger causal answer than any regression. Recognizing that is a high-leverage skill. But they're tools of careful argument, not automatic truth: their credibility is exactly the credibility of their as-if-random assumption, and that must be earned.

The throughline: **natural experiments** find **as-if-random** variation the world produced — a lottery, a policy change, an arbitrary cutoff — that assigned treatment *unrelated to confounders*, giving experiment-grade causal credibility without running an experiment, and attacking observational methods' core weakness (confounding) by finding variation that *couldn't* be confounded. Three canonical designs: **instrumental variables** (an instrument affecting treatment but the outcome *only through* treatment — e.g. the draft lottery; needs the hard-to-verify exclusion restriction), **difference-in-differences** (compare the *change* in treated vs untreated groups around a treatment time, subtracting fixed differences and time trends; needs **parallel trends**), and **regression discontinuity** (an arbitrary **cutoff** makes those just above/below comparable — local randomization; estimate is *local*). They trade "no unmeasured confounding" for **more defensible but still-unprovable design assumptions**, are **opportunistic** (you must find one), give often **local** estimates, and **demand scrutiny** of the as-if-random claim — a genuine middle path between the gold-standard RCT and assumption-heavy adjustment, and a high-leverage skill to *recognize* in your own data.

## Key takeaways

- **Natural experiments** exploit **as-if-random** variation the world provides (a lottery, policy change, arbitrary cutoff) that assigned treatment *unrelated to confounders* — giving experiment-grade credibility without an RCT, and sidestepping confounding (post 6's weakness) by finding variation that *couldn't* be confounded.
- **Instrumental variables (IV)**: use an **instrument** that affects the treatment but the outcome *only through* the treatment and is unrelated to confounders (e.g. draft lottery → military service → earnings); powerful but rests on the hard-to-verify **exclusion restriction**.
- **Difference-in-differences (DiD)**: compare the *change* in the treated group to the *change* in an untreated group around a treatment time, subtracting out fixed group differences and general time trends; key assumption is **parallel trends** (partly checkable pre-treatment).
- **Regression discontinuity (RDD)**: an arbitrary **cutoff** makes units just above/below essentially identical except for treatment — a local randomization at the boundary; credible but gives a **local** estimate (only near the cutoff) and needs a genuinely arbitrary, unmanipulated threshold.
- They trade "no unmeasured confounding" for **more defensible but still-unprovable** design assumptions, are **opportunistic** (must be found, not conjured), often give **local** estimates (IV: compliers; RDD: near cutoff — external-validity limits), and **live or die on the as-if-random claim** — a middle path between the RCT and adjustment, and a high-leverage skill to *recognize* (a region-first rollout, a dated policy change, a threshold-gated feature) in your own data.
