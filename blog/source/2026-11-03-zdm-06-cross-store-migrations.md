# Cross-Store Migrations — Changing Databases Entirely

*Same-store migrations reshape a schema; cross-store migrations change the ground itself — relational to document, single-node to distributed, self-hosted to managed, one vendor to another. Every pattern from this series still applies, but the safety nets of a single database — transactions spanning old and new, one replication stream, one consistency model — are gone, and their replacements have to be built. The backbone of those replacements is change data capture: stop writing to two places, start following one.*

Posts [2](/blog/posts/zdm-02-expand-contract.html)–[5](/blog/posts/zdm-05-cutover-and-rollback.html) built the method inside one store; this post rebuilds it across two — the territory of replatforming projects, with the CDC machinery that [Stream Processing](/blog/series/stream-processing-from-first-principles/) and the [Spanner migration tooling I've contributed to](/blog/posts/cdc-minimal-downtime-spanner-migration.html) both live in.

## CDC replaces dual-writes as the backbone

Post 4 flagged the cross-store dual-write problem: two writes, no shared transaction, divergence by design. The structural fix is to stop having two writers. **Change data capture** tails the source database's own write-ahead/replication log and emits every committed change as an ordered stream; a pipeline applies that stream to the target. One writer (production, unchanged), one follower (the pipeline) — the two-generals gap replaced by a consumer offset. The properties that make CDC the backbone: it captures **what actually committed** (no app-level race can diverge it), it carries **ordering per key** (the log's order), it's **resumable** (offsets checkpoint like post 3's cursors), and it keeps capturing **during** the long initial copy, which solves the hardest sequencing problem — snapshot-then-stream: bulk-copy a consistent snapshot (post 3's batched machinery, pointed across stores), note the log position the snapshot corresponds to, then apply the stream from that position; the target converges to live without a write ever stopping.

The pipeline inherits every verb this series taught: applies are **idempotent** (replays happen), throttled (the target has its own production neighbors), checkpointed, observed — with one new verb: **transform**. Cross-store means the shapes differ — rows to documents, foreign keys to embedded structures, types to their nearest cousins — and the transform carries all of post 1's semantic-drift risk concentrated in one component. It gets the full treatment: reviewed as production code, property-tested on the nasty values (encodings, timezones, nulls, numeric edges), and judged by verification rather than by passing its own tests.

## Verification and cutover, harder edition

Post 4's instruments return with cross-store complications. **Comparison needs a common language** — you can't checksum a Postgres range against a DynamoDB partition directly; the diff sweep compares *canonicalized* representations (apply the transform to the source, compare transformed-source to target), which conveniently also tests the transform continuously. **Shadow reads cross a semantic gap** — the new store may return differently-ordered sets, different precision, different null semantics; the comparator must encode *intended* equivalence, and every "that difference is fine" decision gets written down, because those decisions are the migration's real spec. **Lag is now first-class** — CDC lag is the staleness of the new store; read-cutover ramps (post 5) gate on lag bounds, and write cutover *requires* lag ≈ 0 at the moment of flip (a brief write pause — seconds of queued writes, not downtime — is the honest implementation of the final switch in most real systems; design it, rehearse it, measure it). **Rollback means reverse CDC** — keeping the bridge (post 5) now means a return pipeline from new store back to old, built and tested *before* write cutover, because improvising a reverse replication path during an incident is not a plan. And the dual-stack period costs real money and attention — two databases, two on-call surfaces, a pipeline between — which is why cross-store migrations fail more often by *stalling* (half-migrated for a year) than by breaking; the declared rollback window and dated demolition from post 5 matter double here.

The takeaway: cross-store migration is the series' method with the shared-database safety nets rebuilt — **CDC as the backbone** (one writer, log-ordered follower, snapshot-then-stream convergence), the **transform as the concentrated-risk component** (reviewed, property-tested, continuously judged by canonicalized diffing), verification comparing *intended* equivalence with the fine-print decisions written down, cutover gated on CDC lag with a rehearsed seconds-long write pause at the flip, and rollback as a pre-built reverse pipeline. The dominant failure mode isn't the outage — it's the stall; the cure is the same declared windows and dated exits as ever, enforced against a dual-stack that costs money every week it lives.

## Key takeaways

- Replace dual-writes with **CDC**: tail the source's log — committed truth, per-key ordering, resumable offsets — and **snapshot-then-stream** from a noted log position so the target converges while writes never stop.
- The **transform is the migration's risk concentrate**: production-code review, property tests on encodings/timezones/nulls/numeric edges, and continuous judgment via canonicalized source-vs-target diffing.
- Verification crosses a semantic gap: comparators encode **intended equivalence**, and every tolerated difference is documented — that document is the real spec.
- Cutover gates on **CDC lag**; the final write flip is honestly implemented as a rehearsed, seconds-long pause; rollback = a **reverse CDC pipeline built before it's needed**.
- Cross-store migrations die by **stalling** more than breaking — dual-stack costs weekly; declared windows and dated demolition are the cure, doubly.
