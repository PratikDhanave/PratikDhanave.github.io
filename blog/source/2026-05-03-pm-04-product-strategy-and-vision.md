# Product Strategy and Vision

*Prioritization answers "what should we build next?" — but that question is unanswerable without a prior one: "what are we trying to achieve, and why?" That's strategy and vision. Without them, prioritization becomes a directionless scramble of locally-sensible choices that don't add up to anything. Vision provides the destination; strategy provides the path; and together they turn a stream of features into a coherent product going somewhere. For engineers, understanding strategy explains the "why" behind everything the team does.*

**Product vision** (where you're going) and **product strategy** (how you'll get there) provide the *direction* that makes prioritization and product decisions coherent. This post covers what vision and strategy are, why they matter, what makes strategy good, and how they guide everything else. It's the "big picture" layer of product management — the destination and path that give meaning to the day-to-day decisions (prioritization, what to build). Without them, product work lacks direction.

## Vision: where you're going

A **product vision** is the *long-term aspiration* — where the product is ultimately going, the future it aims to create. It's the destination:

- **The vision is the long-term destination.** A product vision describes the *future state* the product aims for — the meaningful change it wants to make, where it's headed over the long term. It's aspirational and *directional* (the destination), not a detailed plan. The vision answers "where are we ultimately going?" The North Star. The long-term aim.
- **It provides direction and meaning.** The vision gives the team a *shared direction* and *sense of purpose* — a "why" that motivates and aligns. It's the destination that all the work heads toward, giving day-to-day decisions meaning (they serve the vision). A compelling vision inspires and orients the team. Vision provides purpose and direction. Something to head toward.
- **It's stable while tactics change.** The vision is relatively *stable* (the long-term destination doesn't change often), even as the *strategy and tactics* (how you pursue it) adapt. It's the fixed North Star you navigate by while the route flexes. A stable vision with adaptive strategy is the healthy pattern. Stable destination, flexible route. The North Star holds steady.

A product vision is the long-term aspirational destination — where the product is ultimately going — providing shared direction, purpose, and meaning that orients the team, and staying stable while tactics adapt. The vision is *where*; strategy is *how you get there*.

## Strategy: how you'll get there

A **product strategy** is the *plan for achieving the vision* — the approach, choices, and focus that will get you from where you are toward where you're going:

- **Strategy is the path to the vision.** If the vision is the *destination*, the strategy is the *path* — the *approach* for getting there: which customers/markets to focus on, what problems to solve, how to differentiate, what to prioritize. Strategy bridges the gap between *today* and the *vision*. Strategy answers "how will we get to the vision?" The route to the destination. From here to there.
- **Strategy is about choices and focus.** A real strategy makes *choices* — what to focus on and (crucially) what *not* to (which customers, which problems, which approach). Strategy is *deciding what matters and where to concentrate* — a coherent set of choices, not "do everything." Like prioritization at a higher level, strategy is about focus and tradeoffs. Strategy is choosing where to play and how to win. Choices, not everything.
- **It guides lower-level decisions.** The strategy *guides* the prioritization and product decisions below it — what to prioritize (the previous post) follows from the strategy (build what advances the strategic goals). Strategy provides the *criteria* for prioritization and the *coherence* that makes decisions add up. Strategy makes lower-level choices coherent. The frame for prioritization. It gives prioritization its criteria.

A product strategy is the plan for achieving the vision — the path, made of *choices and focus* (what to pursue and not) — that bridges today to the vision and *guides* the lower-level prioritization and product decisions (giving them coherent criteria). Vision is the destination; strategy is the focused path. Together they matter enormously.

## Why vision and strategy matter

Vision and strategy matter because they provide the *direction* without which product work becomes incoherent — worth making explicit:

- **They make prioritization possible and coherent.** Prioritization (the previous post) requires *criteria* — and vision/strategy provide them (prioritize what advances the strategy toward the vision). Without a vision/strategy, prioritization is *directionless* — locally-sensible choices that don't add up. Vision/strategy give prioritization its direction and coherence. No strategy, no coherent prioritization. Direction enables good choices.
- **They align the team.** A shared vision and strategy *align* everyone — the team, stakeholders, and leadership rowing toward the *same* destination via the *same* approach. Without shared direction, effort scatters (everyone pursuing their own idea of what matters). Vision/strategy align effort toward common goals. Alignment around a shared direction. Everyone rowing the same way.
- **They prevent the directionless-feature-factory failure.** A common failure is the *feature factory* — shipping features with no coherent direction, a stream of stuff that doesn't add up to a meaningful product. Vision/strategy prevent this by giving the features a *destination and coherent path* — so the product *goes somewhere* rather than accreting disconnected features. Vision/strategy turn a feature factory into a coherent product. Features with a point. Direction over disconnected output.
- **They give the "why" behind the work.** For everyone (especially engineers), vision/strategy provide the *why* behind what the team builds — the destination and rationale that make the work meaningful and understandable. Understanding the strategy explains *why* you're building what you're building (connecting to the "why" the PM owns). Vision/strategy answer "why are we doing this?" The meaning behind the work.

Vision and strategy matter because they provide the direction that makes prioritization coherent, aligns the team, prevents the directionless feature-factory failure, and gives everyone the "why" behind the work. Without them, product work lacks direction and coherence. So what makes a *good* strategy?

## What makes strategy good

Not all "strategies" are good — a real, effective strategy has qualities worth understanding (and common failures to avoid):

- **A strategy makes real choices (including what not to do).** A good strategy makes *genuine choices* — clearly deciding what to focus on and what *not* to. A "strategy" that tries to do *everything* (no real choices, no focus) isn't a strategy — it's a wish list. Real strategy has focus and says no (like prioritization). Good strategy chooses; bad "strategy" tries to do everything. Choices define strategy. No choices, no strategy.
- **It's grounded in reality (customers, market, capabilities).** Good strategy is grounded in *reality* — real customer needs (the problem post), the market and competition (the GTM series), and your actual capabilities — not wishful thinking. A strategy disconnected from reality (ignoring customers, market, or what you can do) fails. Strategy must fit reality. Grounded, not wishful. Reality-based choices.
- **It's coherent and focused.** A good strategy is *coherent* (its choices fit together toward the vision) and *focused* (concentrated on what matters, not scattered). Coherence and focus are what make a strategy *effective* — a set of aligned, focused choices that reinforce each other toward the goal. Coherent, focused choices. Aligned toward the vision. The pieces fit together.
- **Beware the vision/strategy failures.** Common failures: *no strategy* (just shipping features — the feature factory); *fake strategy* (vague aspirations or a wish list with no real choices); *strategy disconnected from reality* (wishful, ignoring customers/market); or *rigid strategy* (never adapting as you learn). Good strategy avoids these — real choices, grounded in reality, coherent, and adapted as needed (the plan-and-adapt theme). Avoid the strategy failure modes. Real, grounded, coherent, adaptive.

Good product strategy makes real choices (including what not to do — not a do-everything wish list), is grounded in reality (customers, market, capabilities), and is coherent and focused (aligned choices reinforcing each other toward the vision) — avoiding the failures of no strategy (feature factory), fake strategy, disconnected strategy, and rigid strategy. Vision (destination) and strategy (focused, grounded path) provide the direction that gives product management coherence. ## Key takeaways

- A product vision is the long-term aspirational destination — where the product is ultimately going, the meaningful change it aims to make — providing shared direction, purpose, and meaning that orients the team, and staying relatively stable while tactics adapt (a stable North Star with a flexible route).
- A product strategy is the plan for achieving the vision — the *path*, made of choices and focus (what to pursue and, crucially, what *not* to) — that bridges today to the vision and guides the lower-level prioritization and product decisions (giving them coherent criteria).
- Vision and strategy matter because they make prioritization coherent (providing its criteria — without them prioritization is directionless), align the team (rowing toward a shared destination), prevent the directionless "feature factory" failure (features that don't add up), and give everyone (especially engineers) the "why" behind the work.
- Good strategy makes real choices including what *not* to do (a do-everything "strategy" is a wish list, not a strategy), is grounded in reality (real customer needs, market, and your capabilities — not wishful thinking), and is coherent and focused (aligned choices reinforcing each other toward the vision).
- Beware the common failures: no strategy (just shipping features), fake strategy (vague aspirations / a wish list with no real choices), strategy disconnected from reality, and rigid strategy (never adapting) — good strategy is real, grounded, coherent, and adapted as you learn.
