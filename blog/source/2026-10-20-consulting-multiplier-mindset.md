# The Multiplier Mindset: Consulting That Leaves Teams Stronger

*The best outcome of a consulting engagement isn't that the consultant becomes indispensable — it's that the team no longer needs them. Here's the mindset that gets you there, and why it's the right way to do forward-deployed work.*

There's a quiet question worth asking about any consulting engagement: *when it ends, what does it leave behind?*

Two very different answers are possible. One kind of engagement leaves behind a dependency — a system only the consultant fully understands, a process that quietly breaks when they step away, a relationship built on the client needing them to keep coming back. The other leaves behind a stronger team — people who can now do something they couldn't before, a capability that keeps paying off long after the consultant is gone.

I think the second kind is not only the more honorable goal, but the better one in every sense that matters. I've come to call it the multiplier mindset.

## Multipliers and diminishers

The language comes from Liz Wiseman's work on leadership. In her research, she describes two kinds of leaders: *diminishers*, who (often unintentionally) make themselves the center of everything and leave people smaller, and *multipliers*, who amplify the intelligence and capability of everyone around them. A multiplier leaves a room more capable than they found it.

The same distinction applies beautifully to consulting. A diminisher-consultant optimizes — consciously or not — for their own indispensability. A multiplier-consultant optimizes for the client's capability. Both can be talented. Only one leaves the team genuinely better off.

## Why this fits forward deployment especially well

In forward-deployed engineering, the multiplier mindset isn't just a nice ideal — it's the whole point of the role done well.

The goal of embedding with a team to build an AI system on their real data was never to build something only you can operate. It's to prove what's possible, build it *with* the people who'll live with it, and hand over not just working software but the understanding behind it. A deployment that only works while you're standing next to it hasn't really been delivered.

The best forward-deployed work, in other words, is measured by how little the team needs you at the end.

## What the multiplier mindset looks like in practice

A few habits turn the mindset into something real:

- **Build alongside, not in a black box.** Working *with* a team — sharing the reasoning, not just the result — means the capability stays after the engagement does.

- **Hand over understanding, not just artifacts.** Documentation matters, but the deeper gift is the thinking: how to scope the next problem, how to measure whether an AI system is actually good enough to trust, how to tell a reusable pattern from a one-off. Teach the discipline, and the team can repeat the success without you.

- **Turn one win into a repeatable capability.** A multiplier doesn't just solve today's problem; they leave behind a foundation that makes the next ten problems easier. Each engagement should make the team a little more self-sufficient, not a little more dependent.

- **Be honest about what they don't need you for.** Pointing a client toward the simplest path — even when it means less work for you — is one of the clearest signals of a multiplier at work, and one of the fastest ways to earn lasting trust.

## It's also simply good business

It would be easy to frame all this as idealism at the expense of practicality. I don't think the trade-off is real.

Teams remember who left them stronger. That reputation travels — in referrals, in repeat invitations for bigger and more interesting work, in relationships that compound over years rather than ending in quiet resentment. The consultant who makes themselves unnecessary on a small engagement is very often the first person a client calls for the next big one. Generosity with capability turns out to be one of the most durable forms of business development there is.

## The measure that matters

So here's the standard I try to hold myself to: a good engagement is one where, after it ends, the team is more capable than when it began — and would happily work together again, not out of dependence, but out of trust.

If that's the kind of partnership you value — work that leaves your team genuinely stronger — I'd be glad to talk.
