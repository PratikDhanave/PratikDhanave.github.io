# Finding the First Client — How the Work Actually Begins

*The hardest client to find is the first one, and almost none of the advice about it is honest. Here's how forward-deployed consulting work really begins — not with cold outreach and a funnel, but with trust that already exists.*

The question I get asked most by engineers thinking about consulting is some version of: *but how do you get clients?* It's the right question, and the honest answer is less glamorous and more reassuring than the marketing playbooks suggest.

This essay is about where the first engagement actually comes from. I'll try to be truthful rather than aspirational, because the aspirational version — build an audience, run a funnel, close inbound leads — describes year three, not day one.

## The first client is almost never a stranger

Here is the thing nobody selling a course wants to tell you: the first forward-deployed engagement almost always comes from someone who already trusts you. A former colleague who became a founder. A manager who moved to a new company and remembers what you shipped. Someone you helped on a hard problem years ago who never forgot it.

This isn't a lucky accident. It's structural. Forward-deployed work asks a client to let you deep inside their environment, their data, their team. That's an enormous amount of trust to extend, and strangers don't extend it on the strength of a pitch. They extend it because someone they believe can vouch for you — or because they watched you do good work themselves.

So the real answer to "how do you find the first client" is: *you found them years ago, by being someone worth remembering.* The groundwork for your consulting practice was laid in every job where you left people stronger than you found them.

## What you're actually selling

Early on, I made the mistake of thinking I was selling expertise — a resume of technologies, a list of things I knew. That framing almost never lands, because the client can't evaluate your expertise and doesn't want to try. What they can evaluate is whether they trust you to be useful inside their mess.

So what you're really offering, especially at the start, is a combination of:

- **Demonstrated judgment** — evidence that you've navigated the kind of ambiguity they're facing and made good calls.
- **Low-risk entry** — a way for them to find out if you're helpful without betting the company on it.
- **Genuine interest in their problem** — not a template you're trying to apply, but real curiosity about what's hard for *them*.

Notice that none of these is a sales technique. They're all things you either have or don't, built over years. The "finding" is mostly a matter of making it easy for the trust that already exists to turn into a conversation.

## Making yourself findable

That said, luck favors the visible. A few things genuinely help the first client find you, and they compound:

- **Write about the work.** Not to go viral, but so that when someone who already half-trusts you goes looking, they find evidence of how you think. This series is partly that. A body of honest writing is the cheapest, most durable form of credibility there is.
- **Tell people, plainly, what you're doing now.** Most first engagements are lost not to competition but to nobody knowing you were available. When I decided to [open my practice](/blog/posts/opening-my-consulting-practice.html), simply saying so — clearly, without hype — did more than any clever outreach.
- **Stay generous before there's a deal.** The person you help with a thirty-minute answer, expecting nothing, is often the person who refers you six months later. Generosity isn't a tactic, but it does travel.

## Start before you feel ready

The last thing I'd say about the first client is that you will not feel ready, and that's fine. Waiting until you have a polished brand, a service catalog, and a pipeline is a way of never starting. The first engagement teaches you more about your practice than a year of planning.

I started with a great deal of excitement and real humility, one conversation with someone who already trusted me, and a willingness to prove value by building something real rather than talking about it. That turned out to be enough to begin — and beginning is the only thing that leads to the second client, and the tenth.
