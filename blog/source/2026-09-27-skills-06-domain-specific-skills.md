# Domain-Specific Skills

*Where general business skills capture how you do ordinary work, domain-specific skills capture deep expertise in a particular field — the specialized knowledge, rules, and judgment of law, medicine, finance, a specific codebase, a regulated process. These are the highest-value and highest-stakes skills: they let an agent operate in a specialized domain, but they demand the most rigor, because being wrong in a specialized domain is both more likely and more costly. This post is about capturing deep domain expertise as skills.*

Post 5 covered broadly-applicable business skills; this post covers the opposite end — deep, specialized expertise. Domain-specific skills are where an agent becomes genuinely useful in a field that requires real knowledge, and where the design discipline (specificity, accuracy, grounding) matters most. They're also where skills connect to the blog's deep domain content (fintech, AI governance, security).

## What domain-specific skills capture

**Domain-specific skills** encode the specialized knowledge and practice of a particular field, giving an agent capability in work that requires expertise the general model lacks or holds only shallowly:

- **Specialized domain knowledge** — the facts, concepts, rules, and terminology of a field: the specifics of a regulation, the structure of a financial instrument, the protocol of a medical procedure, the architecture of a particular system. Deep, particular knowledge general training covers thinly or not at all.
- **Domain rules and constraints** — the hard rules the domain imposes: regulatory requirements, compliance constraints, safety rules, the "you must/must never" of the field. These are where domain work is most unforgiving.
- **Domain-specific procedure and judgment** — how experts actually *do* the specialized work: the method, the decision criteria, the heuristics, the way a seasoned practitioner approaches it. This is the hard-won expertise that distinguishes a specialist from a generalist.
- **Specific-system / specific-context knowledge** — often the most valuable: deep knowledge of *your* specific codebase, *your* specific systems, *your* specific regulatory context — particulars no general model can know, which turn a generic specialist into one that works in *your* environment.

These overlap with general business skills in form (both capture specifics) but differ in *depth and stakes*: domain-specific skills go deep into a field's expertise, and the work they enable is often specialized, consequential, and hard to verify — a financial analysis, a compliance check, a medical-adjacent task, a change to a complex system. That depth and those stakes drive everything about how carefully they must be built.

## Why they demand the most rigor

Domain-specific skills are high-value *and* high-stakes, and the stakes raise the design bar sharply (connecting to the blog's AI-governance and AI-financial-engineering care):

- **Accuracy is critical and errors are costly.** In a specialized domain, a wrong fact, a misapplied rule, or a subtle misjudgment can cause real harm — a compliance violation, a financial loss, a safety issue. The tolerance for the agent being confidently wrong is far lower than in casual tasks, so domain skills must be built for *accuracy*, not just helpfulness.
- **The model's domain knowledge is shaky.** General models hold specialized knowledge shallowly and can **hallucinate** domain facts and rules convincingly — inventing a regulation, misstating an instrument's terms, confidently asserting a wrong procedure. This is exactly where LLM hallucination is most dangerous (the AI-financial-engineering and governance series' point), so domain skills can't rely on the model's parametric knowledge — they must *supply* the authoritative domain knowledge and *ground* the agent in it.
- **Grounding over generation.** The right pattern (echoing document-AI practice): a domain skill should **provide the authoritative domain knowledge** (the actual rules, the specific facts, the real procedure) and instruct the agent to work *from that*, rather than from its own shaky recollection. Ground the agent in the correct domain material (and ideally its source) so it reasons from truth, not from a plausible-sounding hallucination. For high-stakes domains, this grounding is the difference between a usable skill and a dangerous one.
- **Verification and human oversight.** Because domain work is consequential and hard to check, domain skills should build in verification — cite sources, show reasoning, and keep a human expert in the loop for high-stakes outputs (the augment-not-replace principle, strongest here). A domain skill makes the expert faster and more consistent; it doesn't replace the expert's accountability for specialized, consequential judgments.

The design implication: domain-specific skills demand the most care of any skill — authoritative grounded knowledge (not the model's guesses), explicit rules and constraints stated precisely, accuracy built in and verified, and human oversight for high stakes. The specificity discipline of post 3 applies with full force, plus a heavy emphasis on *correctness and grounding* that general business skills need less. A sloppy domain skill isn't just unhelpful — in a specialized, consequential field it's actively risky.

## Building and using domain skills well

Practical guidance for domain-specific skills, pulling the series' threads together:

- **Capture authoritative knowledge, not summaries.** Build the skill on the *actual* domain material — the real regulation text, the specific system documentation, the authoritative reference — not a paraphrase the agent might further distort. Ground in sources, and preserve provenance where it matters (the AI-financial-engineering lesson).
- **State rules precisely and prescriptively.** Domain rules and constraints should be stated exactly ("must X," "never Y," "if Z then W"), not left to the agent's judgment, because the domain's hard rules are where error is most costly (post 3's "prescriptive where reliability matters," amplified).
- **Lean on your specific context — it's the unique value.** The highest-value domain skills often encode *your* specific systems, codebase, regulatory situation, or practices — the particulars no general model and no competitor's generic tool can have. This is where domain skills are most defensible and most useful: a generic "finance" skill is replaceable; a skill encoding *your* firm's specific instruments and processes is not.
- **Respect the context budget even with deep knowledge** (post 2). Domain skills risk bloat (lots of knowledge to include), so use progressive disclosure hard — load the essential rules/procedure up front, pull detailed reference on demand — to keep deep knowledge available without flooding context.
- **Verify, cite, and keep the expert accountable.** For consequential domain work, build in source citation and human review; the skill accelerates and standardizes the expert, who remains accountable for the specialized judgment.

The synthesis: domain-specific skills are where agents become capable in fields that demand real expertise — and where the discipline of the whole series culminates. They capture deep, specific, often organization-particular knowledge and practice; they demand the most rigor because specialized errors are likely (shaky model knowledge, hallucination) and costly (consequential work); and they're built on *grounded authoritative knowledge, precisely-stated rules, context-budget discipline, and verification with human oversight*. Done right, a domain-specific skill lets an agent do genuinely specialized work reliably in *your* context — the highest expression of what skills can do, and the one that most demands getting the craft right. ## Key takeaways

- **Domain-specific skills** encode deep field expertise: specialized **knowledge** (facts/concepts/rules/terminology general training covers thinly), hard **rules and constraints** (regulatory/compliance/safety "must/never"), expert **procedure and judgment**, and — often most valuable — **your specific codebase/systems/regulatory context** (particulars no general model or competitor's tool can have).
- They're **high-value *and* high-stakes**, which raises the design bar: **accuracy is critical** (specialized errors cause compliance violations, financial loss, safety issues — low tolerance for confident wrongness) and the **model's domain knowledge is shaky and hallucination-prone** (it invents regulations/terms/procedures convincingly — most dangerous exactly here).
- So domain skills must **supply authoritative knowledge and ground the agent in it** (work *from* the real rules/facts/procedure, not from shaky parametric recall — grounding over generation is the difference between usable and dangerous), **state rules precisely and prescriptively** ("must X / never Y / if Z then W"), and build in **verification + human oversight** (cite sources, keep the expert accountable — augment-not-replace, strongest here).
- Build them on **authoritative sources** (not distortable paraphrases; preserve provenance), **lean on your specific context** (the defensible, unique value — generic domain skills are replaceable; skills encoding *your* firm's specifics are not), and respect the **context budget** with hard progressive disclosure (load essential rules up front, pull deep reference on demand — post 2).
- Domain-specific skills are where **agents become reliably capable in fields demanding real expertise** and where the series' discipline culminates (specificity + grounding + precise rules + context budget + verification) — the highest expression of skills, and the one most demanding the craft; a sloppy domain skill in a consequential field is actively risky, not just unhelpful.
