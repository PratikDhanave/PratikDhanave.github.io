# Why People Are Everything

*Strip a company down to its essence and what remains isn't the product, the code, or the strategy — it's the people who create all of those. An organization is its people, which makes attracting, developing, and keeping good people arguably the highest-leverage thing any company does. Yet engineers and technical leaders often treat "people stuff" as someone else's job (HR's), missing that hiring and developing a team is a core skill that shapes everything they can achieve. Understanding people — how to hire, grow, and keep them — is understanding how organizations actually succeed.*

This series is a practical guide to **hiring and people** — how to attract, assess, onboard, develop, and retain the people who make an organization work. It's aimed at engineers and technical leaders who hire, manage, or want to build great teams (or understand how good teams are built). This first post frames why people matter so much — arguably the highest-leverage area — why engineers should care, and the scope of the series (hiring, interviewing, onboarding, feedback, growth, retention, team-building). Because in the end, an organization is its people.

## An organization is its people

The foundational truth: **an organization is fundamentally its people** — everything a company does is done *by* people, so the people *are* the company:

- **People do everything.** Everything an organization accomplishes — building products, serving customers, executing strategy — is done *by people*. The product, the code, the results are all *created by the people*. So the *people* are the organization's *core* — its capability *is* its people's capability. An org is its people's abilities. People are the company. Everything runs on them.
- **Better people, better outcomes.** Since people do everything, the *quality* of an organization's people largely determines the quality of its outcomes — *great people* build great things; *the wrong people* struggle regardless of everything else. The team you have *is* what you can achieve. Better people yield better outcomes. Talent shapes results.
- **People are the real competitive advantage.** In most organizations, the *people* (their skills, knowledge, and how they work together) are the deepest *competitive advantage* — harder to replicate than products or strategy (which competitors can copy). A great team is a durable edge. People are often the real moat. The hardest advantage to copy.

An organization is fundamentally its people — they do everything, their quality determines outcomes, and they're often the real competitive advantage (harder to copy than products or strategy). Because the people *are* the company, attracting, developing, and keeping good people is enormously consequential — arguably the highest-leverage area.

## People as the highest-leverage area

Because an organization is its people, *hiring and developing people* is arguably the **highest-leverage** thing a company (or leader) does:

- **Hiring is high-leverage.** *Who* you hire has *outsized, compounding* impact — a great hire contributes enormously (and helps hire/build more great people); a bad hire drags the team (and can do lasting damage). Getting hiring *right* is one of the *highest-leverage* activities, because it determines the *people* who determine everything. Hiring is a top-leverage activity. Who you hire shapes everything.
- **Developing people multiplies capability.** *Developing* the people you have (growth, learning — later posts) *multiplies* their capability over time — a team that grows becomes far more capable. Investing in people's growth is high-leverage (it compounds). Developing people compounds capability. Growth multiplies the team.
- **Keeping good people protects the leverage.** *Retaining* good people (later post) protects all the value of hiring and developing them — losing good people (turnover) is *costly* (lost capability, knowledge, and the cost of replacing them). Keeping good people preserves the high-leverage investment. Retention protects the leverage. Don't lose what you built.
- **People decisions have lasting impact.** People decisions (who to hire, how to develop and treat them) have *lasting, compounding* impact — building (or eroding) the team's capability over time. Because these decisions compound and are hard to reverse, they're worth great care. People decisions compound and last. High stakes, long shadow.

Hiring and developing people is arguably the highest-leverage area — who you hire has outsized compounding impact, developing people multiplies capability, keeping them protects the value, and people decisions compound and last. Since the people *are* the company, getting people right is among the most consequential things a leader does. Which is why engineers and technical leaders should care.

## Why engineers and technical leaders should care

Engineers and technical leaders often *underrate* "people stuff" (as HR's job) — but understanding people is a core skill that shapes what they can achieve:

- **Technical leaders build and lead teams.** As engineers grow into *leadership* (tech leads, managers, founders), *building and leading teams* becomes central — hiring, developing, and retaining people. Understanding people isn't optional for technical leaders; it's a *core part of the job* (arguably the most important part). Technical leaders must understand people. Leading is mostly people.
- **Engineers participate in hiring.** Even non-leaders *participate* in hiring (interviewing candidates — the interviewing post) — and doing it *well* (assessing effectively, avoiding bias) matters (bad interviewing yields bad hires). Understanding hiring makes engineers *better interviewers* and contributors to team-building. Engineers help hire; do it well. Interviewing is a real skill.
- **Team quality shapes your work and success.** The *team* you're on (its people, how it works) profoundly shapes your *work experience and success* — great teammates and a well-built team make everything better; a poorly-built team makes everything harder. Understanding what makes teams great helps you contribute to (and choose) good ones. Team quality shapes your experience. Your team is your world.
- **It's underrated by technical people.** Engineers often *underrate* people/hiring (as "soft" or HR's domain) — a mistake, since people are the *highest-leverage* area (above) and building great teams is a *learnable, crucial* skill. Taking people seriously (as seriously as technical skills) is a mark of growing into effective technical leadership. Don't underrate people skills. People matter as much as tech.

Engineers and technical leaders should care about people because technical leaders build and lead teams (a core part of the job), engineers participate in hiring (better done well), team quality shapes their work and success, and it's an underrated but high-leverage, learnable skill. Taking people seriously is essential to effective technical leadership. The series covers how.

## Key takeaways

- An organization is fundamentally its people — everything a company accomplishes (products, code, results, strategy execution) is done *by* people, so the people *are* the company's core capability; better people yield better outcomes, and people are often the real competitive advantage (harder to replicate than products or strategy).
- Because the people are the company, hiring and developing people is arguably the highest-leverage area — who you hire has outsized, compounding impact (a great hire contributes enormously and helps build more; a bad hire drags and damages), developing people multiplies capability over time, and retaining good people protects that value.
- People decisions (who to hire, how to develop and treat them) compound and last (building or eroding team capability over time, hard to reverse) — so they're worth great care.
- Engineers and technical leaders should care because technical leaders build and lead teams (a core, arguably most-important part of the job), engineers participate in hiring (better done well — bad interviewing yields bad hires), the team's quality profoundly shapes their work and success, and it's an underrated but high-leverage, learnable skill.
- The series covers the people lifecycle — the hiring process, interviewing/assessment, onboarding, feedback/performance, growth/career development, retention/engagement, and building a great team — helping engineers and technical leaders build and be part of great teams (arguably the most important thing they can do).
