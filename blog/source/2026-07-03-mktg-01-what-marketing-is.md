# What Marketing Actually Is

*Engineers tend to hold marketing in quiet contempt — associating it with spam, hype, manipulation, and the dishonest inflation of mediocre products. That contempt is understandable and mostly aimed at bad marketing, which is real and everywhere. But it causes a costly blind spot: dismissing marketing as a discipline means your genuinely good work goes undiscovered, out-competed by worse products that were merely better explained. Marketing, done well, isn't manipulation — it's the honest work of helping the right people understand and find something valuable.*

This series is a practical guide to **marketing for technical products** — aimed at engineers and technical founders who build things and need people to know about, understand, and adopt them. It's the marketing-discipline complement to the strategy-level [Go-to-Market series](/blog/series/go-to-market-strategy/). This first post confronts the engineer's disdain for marketing, defines what marketing actually is, distinguishes its major disciplines, and reframes it as honest and essential rather than dishonest and optional.

## The engineer's problem with marketing

Many technical people have a genuine aversion to marketing, and it's worth taking seriously rather than dismissing — because the aversion contains a truth *and* a costly error:

- **The truth: bad marketing is real and awful.** Much of what people experience as "marketing" *is* bad — spam, hype, manipulation, dishonest claims, dark patterns, inflating weak products with slick messaging. Engineers rightly dislike this. The contempt is a reasonable reaction to real, pervasive bad marketing.
- **The error: concluding marketing itself is bad.** The mistake is generalizing from bad marketing to *all* marketing — deciding the whole discipline is dishonest and beneath you. This is like judging all code by the worst spaghetti you've seen. Bad marketing is bad; *marketing* is a neutral discipline that can be done honestly or dishonestly, well or badly.
- **The cost of the blind spot.** Dismissing marketing means neglecting it — and neglected marketing means your good work goes *undiscovered*. The painful reality (from the GTM series): superior products routinely lose to inferior ones that were better marketed. If you build something excellent but refuse to market it, you've built something the world won't find. The disdain, left unexamined, actively harms your own work.

The reframe this series offers: **the problem isn't marketing, it's *bad* marketing** — and the answer isn't to avoid marketing but to do it *well and honestly*. Good marketing is entirely compatible with integrity; in fact, honesty is a *strength* in marketing (below). Getting past the reflexive disdain — recognizing marketing as a legitimate, learnable, honest discipline — is the first step to letting your good work be found.

## What marketing actually is

Stripped of the negative associations, **marketing is the work of connecting a valuable product with the people who would benefit from it** — helping the right people become aware of it, understand its value, and choose it. Its honest core:

- **It's about understanding and communicating value.** At its heart, good marketing is (1) deeply *understanding* your customers and their needs, and (2) *communicating* how your product meets those needs, to the people who have them. It's a bridge between what you built and the people who need it — built on understanding customers and communicating clearly, not on tricks.
- **It's broader than advertising.** "Marketing" is not synonymous with ads (or spam). Advertising is one small, visible slice. Marketing encompasses understanding the market, positioning the product, communicating its value across many channels, building awareness and trust, and enabling people to find and choose it. Equating marketing with ads is like equating engineering with typing.
- **Good marketing serves the customer.** Done well, marketing *helps* customers — it helps people with a problem discover a solution that genuinely helps them, and understand whether it's right for them. There's nothing dishonest about helping the right people find something valuable to them. Marketing becomes dishonest only when it misrepresents — promoting things that *don't* deliver, or deceiving. Honest marketing of a genuinely good product is a service, not a manipulation.

So marketing is the honest discipline of connecting valuable products with the people who need them — through understanding customers and clearly communicating value. This definition is a world away from spam and hype, and it's the version worth learning. The rest of the series is about doing *this* — honest, effective marketing of technical products — well.

## The disciplines of marketing

"Marketing" is an umbrella over several distinct disciplines, and knowing them clarifies what marketing actually involves (they map to the rest of the series):

- **Brand marketing** — building how your product/company is *perceived* over time: its identity, reputation, and the trust and associations people hold. Brand is the long-term, compounding perception that makes people recognize, remember, and trust you (post two).
- **Product marketing** — the bridge between the product and the market: defining positioning and messaging, communicating what the product does and why it matters, launching it, and enabling sales. Product marketing translates the product into value the market understands (post three).
- **Content marketing** — creating valuable content (articles, guides, docs, videos) that attracts, educates, and builds trust with your audience — especially powerful for technical products (post four), including via SEO.
- **Demand generation** — actively creating awareness and interest that fills the funnel with potential customers, through campaigns across channels (post five).
- **Growth marketing** — data-driven, experimental marketing focused on growing users/customers, including growth loops and product-led growth (post seven).

```text
   Brand         — long-term perception, trust, identity
   Product mktg  — positioning, messaging, launches (product ↔ market)
   Content       — attract/educate/build trust via valuable content + SEO
   Demand gen    — create awareness/interest, fill the funnel
   Growth        — experimental, data-driven user/customer growth
```

These disciplines work together (brand builds trust that makes demand gen work; content fuels both; product marketing shapes the message all of them carry), but they're distinct skills and functions. Understanding that "marketing" spans this range — not just ads — is part of taking it seriously as a discipline. The series covers each, plus the specific case of *developer marketing* (marketing to engineers, post six) and *measurement* (post eight).

## Honesty as a strength

A final reframe that should reassure the skeptical engineer: in marketing, **honesty isn't just ethically right — it's strategically effective**, especially for technical products and audiences:

- **Trust is the foundation, and dishonesty destroys it.** Good marketing builds *trust*, and trust is built on delivering what you promise. Dishonest marketing (overclaiming, hype that the product doesn't back up) destroys trust the moment reality disappoints — and lost trust is extremely hard to rebuild. Honest marketing that accurately represents a good product builds durable trust; dishonest marketing wins a sale and loses a customer (and their word-of-mouth).
- **Technical audiences punish hype hardest.** Engineers and technical buyers are *especially* allergic to hype and quick to detect and reject dishonesty (post six develops this). For technical products, honest, substantive, accurate marketing isn't just nicer — it's the *only* kind that works, because your audience will see through anything else. This is good news for honest builders: your integrity is an asset with this audience.
- **Good marketing of a good product is a virtuous match.** The honest ideal: build something genuinely valuable, then honestly help the people who'd benefit find and understand it. That's marketing you can do with full integrity — and it's the most effective kind, because it's backed by a real product and builds real trust. Marketing and honesty aren't in tension; the best marketing depends on honesty.

Marketing, properly understood, is the honest and essential discipline of connecting valuable products with the people who need them — through understanding customers and communicating value across brand, product, content, demand, and growth. The engineer's disdain targets *bad* marketing; the answer is to do it *well and honestly*, which for technical audiences is the only kind that works. Don't let good work go undiscovered. Next: brand and positioning — the foundation of how you're perceived.

## Key takeaways

- The engineer's disdain for marketing targets *bad* marketing (spam, hype, manipulation — which is real and pervasive), but generalizing that to *all* marketing is a costly error: neglecting marketing means your good work goes undiscovered and loses to worse-but-better-marketed products (the problem is bad marketing, not marketing).
- Marketing is the honest work of connecting a valuable product with the people who'd benefit — understanding customers and their needs, then communicating how the product meets them — and it's far broader than advertising (ads are one small slice); done well, it *serves* customers by helping them find and understand a genuinely useful solution.
- Marketing spans distinct disciplines: brand (long-term perception/trust), product marketing (positioning/messaging/launches — the product↔market bridge), content marketing (attract/educate via valuable content + SEO), demand generation (create awareness, fill the funnel), and growth marketing (experimental user/customer growth) — which work together but are distinct skills.
- Honesty is a strategic strength in marketing, not just an ethical one: trust is the foundation and dishonesty destroys it the moment reality disappoints (winning a sale but losing the customer and their word-of-mouth), and technical audiences especially detect and punish hype — so honest, accurate marketing is the *only* kind that works for technical products.
- The honest ideal — build something genuinely valuable, then honestly help the right people find and understand it — is marketing an engineer can do with full integrity, and it's also the most effective kind because it's backed by a real product and builds durable trust.
