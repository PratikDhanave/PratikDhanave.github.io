Revenue Integrity — Reconciliation, Recognition, and the Metrics Finance Will Compute Anyway

The billing system's last responsibility is proving itself: that every invoice matches money that actually moved, that revenue is recognized when it's earned rather than when it's charged, and that the metrics the business runs on — MRR, churn, net revenue retention — derive from billing data by defined rules rather than by whichever spreadsheet ran last. Revenue integrity is where billing engineering meets accounting, and the systems that skip it get audited into building it retroactively.

Revenue integrity closes the series: reconciliation across billing, processor, and settlement with a short owned exception queue; revenue recognition (charging is not earning — emit service periods and linkage so deferral is computable); and MRR with movements as versioned derivations from the state machine itself.

Testing Billing Systems — Simulated Clocks and Golden Invoices

Billing's defining property — post 1 called it time-dominance — makes it nearly untestable by ordinary means: the interesting behavior happens at renewals, trial expiries, grace-window closures, and anniversary boundaries, none of which occur during a test run unless the test controls time itself. The teams that trust their billing systems all converged on the same answer: make the clock an injectable dependency, simulate months in milliseconds, and pin correctness with golden invoices.

Billing becomes testable when time becomes an injected dependency: simulated clocks drive month-long lifecycles in milliseconds, golden invoices pin the money to the cent with diffs as review artifacts, re-derivation audits check production continuously, and property tests cover the proration arithmetic.

Deprecation — Retiring API Versions Without Burning Your Integrators

Shipping a version is a product launch; retiring one is a diplomatic mission. The clients still on it are, by definition, the ones who didn't upgrade when asked — working code owned by teams with no budget for your roadmap — and behind every deprecation that went badly is an integrator who found out from an outage. Deprecation done well is a designed process: telemetry that knows who's affected, communication that reaches them, migration paths that respect their economics, and sunsets that mean what they say.

Deprecation as designed lifecycle: deprecated-but-fully-functional with machine-readable marking, declared minimum support windows, warning cadences escalating through pre-announced brownouts, account-level telemetry driving targeted comms and burndown curves — and an explicit slip policy protecting sunset credibility.

Model Evolution II: Test-Time Training and the Continual Learning Problem

Post 5 asked where the training signal comes from; this post asks when the weights change — and the two most radical answers bracket the question. Test-time training updates weights during inference, per input, then throws the update away; continual learning updates weights forever and keeps them. One treats adaptation as disposable, the other as permanent — and between them sits the oldest unsolved problem in neural networks: learning the new without trampling the old.

Test-time training adapts weights per input and discards the update — the deepest locus at the narrowest closure; continual learning keeps updates and collides with catastrophic interference, managed by replay, regularization, and isolation. Underneath both: the stability–plasticity dilemma and the two-timescale resolution that meets the memory series.

Memory in Production — Privacy, Forgetting, Isolation, and Proving It Works

A memory system that delights in a demo becomes a liability register in production: it accumulates personal data by design, remembers things users expected to expire, must never leak across tenants, and silently decays in quality unless something measures it. This closing post covers the four disciplines that separate a production memory system from a demo with persistence — privacy, deletion, isolation, and evaluation — and ends where post 1 began: with judgment about what's worth remembering at all.

Production memory is a personal-data system: minimization as selection, answerable 'what do you remember about me', sensitivity tiers, deletion that cascades along provenance edges, hard tenant isolation (memory writes are an injection surface), and layered evals ending in the ablation that proves memory earns its cost. Remembering well is valuable; forgetting well is trustworthy. Closes the series.

Entitlements — Decoupling What Customers Can Do from What They Pay

Scattered through most SaaS codebases is the same bug factory: `if plan == "pro"` checks guarding features. They work until the first pricing change, when "pro" splits into three plans, grandfathered customers keep old capabilities, sales grants an exception, and a trial needs temporary access — and suddenly product code is re-implementing billing logic badly. Entitlements are the missing layer: a dedicated system answering "may this customer do X?" so that product code never asks about plans at all.

Entitlements decouple what customers can do from what they pay: features (boolean, limit, quota), grants with sources and validity, and resolution behind one fast cached check — with plans demoted to versioned bundles. Pricing re-packages freely without product deploys, and exceptions become auditable.
