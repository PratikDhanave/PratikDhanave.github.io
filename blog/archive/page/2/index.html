Latent Diffusion — Why Stable Diffusion Doesn't Diffuse Pixels

The single decision most responsible for putting image generation on consumer GPUs was not a better denoiser or a faster sampler — it was moving the entire diffusion process out of pixel space. Latent diffusion compresses images roughly 48× with a learned autoencoder, runs every denoising step in that compressed space, and decodes once at the end. Same theory as posts 2–5, radically different economics: this is the architecture decision that made Stable Diffusion stable to run.

Stable Diffusion doesn't diffuse pixels. A perceptually-trained autoencoder compresses images ~48×, the entire diffusion apparatus runs unchanged on latents, and a decoder re-synthesizes texture once at the end. The economics, the three-piece architecture, the autoencoder-as-quality-ceiling caveat, and why video inherited the pattern.

Learning to Denoise — DDPM and the Loss That Trains Every Image Model

The reverse of destruction is generation — but only if a network can learn to run one small step of the destruction backwards. The surprise at the heart of DDPM is how plain the training recipe turned out to be: corrupt an image, ask the network which noise was added, score it with mean squared error. No adversary, no instability — a regression loss so simple it reads like a tutorial exercise, hiding a derivation that connects it to maximum likelihood.

Small Gaussian forward steps make each reverse step approximately Gaussian — so generation reduces to regression. DDPM's recipe: predict the added noise, score it with plain mean squared error, sample by walking the chain backwards with fresh noise injected each step. Four lines of training loop, and why they beat the trilemma.
