Expand/Contract — The Only Schema Change Pattern You Need

Every zero-downtime schema change — rename a column, change a type, split a table, add a constraint — is the same move performed carefully: add the new shape alongside the old, migrate readers and writers in compatible steps, then remove the old shape long after anything needs it. Expand/contract (also traveling as "parallel change") is less a technique than the grammar all the techniques are written in. Learn it once and every migration becomes a sentence in it.

Expand/contract is the grammar of zero-downtime schema change: add alongside, migrate in compatible single-step deploys (write-both, read-new, write-new-only), contract late and deliberately. Plus the per-engine fine print: concurrent index builds, declare-then-validate constraints, and lock timeouts as the universal seatbelt.

Delivery Semantics — At-Least-Once, Retries, and the Exactly-Once Myth

Every delivery guarantee is a bet about failure, and webhook systems inherit the oldest result in distributed messaging: between two parties over an unreliable channel, you can deliver at-most-once or at-least-once, but exactly-once delivery is not on the menu — only exactly-once effect, manufactured by the receiver. This post builds that result from first principles and derives the retry machinery both sides need: schedules, backoff, jitter, and the acknowledgment discipline that makes them work.

A webhook POST is the two-generals problem in miniature: the ack can always be lost, so the choices are at-most-once (silent loss) or at-least-once (loud duplicates) — and providers rightly choose loud. The retry machinery that makes it livable: exponential backoff, jitter, bounded published horizons, per-endpoint isolation.

Why Migrations Break Things — The Downtime Taxonomy

"Run the migration at 2 a.m." was a plan when users slept somewhere. For a system that's always on, every schema change, backfill, and datastore move must happen underneath live traffic — and the ways that goes wrong are surprisingly few and surprisingly repeatable: locks that queue the world, long transactions that starve it, mismatched assumptions between old code and new schema. This series is the discipline of changing data out from under a running system; this post is the map of how it breaks.

Migration incidents come in five repeatable shapes: lock queueing, resource starvation, long-transaction side effects, code-schema mismatch across rolling deploys, and silent semantic drift. Against them, three vows: no data-proportional locks, reversibility until a deliberate late point of no return, and proof over belief. Opens the series.

Why Webhooks Are Hard — The Contract Nobody Reads

Webhooks look like the easiest integration pattern in software: the provider POSTs you JSON when something happens. Then production arrives — events show up twice, out of order, late, or not at all; a deploy drops an hour of deliveries; a signature check fails silently — and the team discovers that webhooks are a distributed system whose other half they don't control. This series is the missing engineering discipline for both sides of that contract.

The real webhook contract: at-least-once, unordered, bounded retries, latency-sensitive, point-in-time payloads — loose by necessity, not negligence. The provider delivers possibilities; the receiver establishes truth. Webhooks are state synchronization over an unreliable channel, and this series is the missing discipline for both sides.
