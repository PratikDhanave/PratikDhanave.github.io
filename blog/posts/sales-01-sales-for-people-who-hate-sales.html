Sales for People Who Hate Sales

No word makes an engineer wince quite like "sales." It conjures the pushy car salesman, the manipulative closer, the person who'll say anything to hit quota — everything a truth-valuing technical person recoils from. But that caricature is bad sales, and confusing it with sales itself leaves engineers unable to do something essential: help another person understand that what you've built genuinely solves their problem. Done honestly, that isn't manipulation. It's a service, and it's a learnable skill.

This series is a practical guide to sales and presales for engineers — aimed at technical people who find sales distasteful but need to understand it: to sell their own product as a founder, to work alongside sales, or to move into a technical-selling role. This first post confronts the engineer’s disdain for sales, reframes what honest sales actually is, and makes the case that understanding sales is essential — and entirely compatible with integrity. It parallels the Marketing series’ opening, because sales and marketing share the same image problem and the same honest reality.

The engineer’s disdain for sales

Engineers often hold sales in even lower regard than marketing, and — as with marketing — the disdain contains a truth and a costly error:

  • The truth: bad, pushy sales is real and off-putting. The stereotype exists because pushy, manipulative, dishonest sales is real — pressure tactics, saying anything to close, selling people things they don’t need. Engineers, who value truth and dislike being manipulated, rightly find this repellent. The contempt is a fair reaction to genuinely bad sales.
  • The error: thinking all sales is that. The mistake is concluding that sales itself is inherently manipulative and beneath you. But pushy manipulation is bad sales — and, notably, not even effective sales in most modern contexts (it destroys the trust that real selling depends on). Sales is a discipline that can be done honestly and well, or dishonestly and badly. The caricature is the bad version, not the thing itself.
  • The cost: your good work goes unsold. Refusing to engage with sales means, if you’re a founder or building anything, your genuinely good product doesn’t reach the people it could help — because someone has to help customers understand and decide to adopt it, and that’s selling. Disdaining all sales leaves your work unsold and undiscovered, the same self-defeating blind spot as disdaining marketing.

The reframe: the problem is bad sales, not sales — and the answer is to understand and do sales honestly and well, which (as with marketing) is the only kind that works sustainably anyway. Getting past the reflexive disdain is the first step to being able to help your good work reach the people who need it.

What honest sales actually is

Stripped of the pushy caricature, sales is the process of helping a customer understand whether and how your product solves their problem, and helping them decide to adopt it. Its honest core:

  • It’s helping someone make a good decision. Good sales isn’t pushing a product on someone — it’s understanding the customer’s problem and helping them see whether your solution genuinely addresses it, so they can make a good decision. When the product fits their need, helping them recognize that and adopt it serves them. Sales, done right, is a helping profession.
  • It’s built on understanding, not persuasion tricks. Modern effective sales is far more about understanding the customer (their problem, situation, needs) than about clever persuasion or pressure. The best salespeople listen more than they talk, diagnose before prescribing, and genuinely try to solve the customer’s problem (the consultative/solution-selling approach, a later post). It’s closer to a doctor diagnosing than a huckster pitching.
  • It’s honest by necessity. As with marketing to technical audiences, honest sales is not just ethical but effective — especially for considered purchases and technical buyers. Dishonest selling (overpromising, pressuring people into bad fits) destroys trust, produces unhappy customers who churn and warn others, and doesn’t survive. Sustainable sales requires honesty. Selling a genuinely good product to people it genuinely helps, honestly, is entirely compatible with integrity.

So honest sales is helping the right customers understand and decide to adopt a product that genuinely solves their problem — built on understanding them, not on manipulation. This is a world away from the pushy stereotype, and it’s the version worth learning. It’s also, crucially, complementary to marketing.

Sales vs marketing

Sales and marketing are related but distinct, and understanding the difference clarifies what sales specifically is:

  • Marketing is one-to-many; sales is one-to-one. Marketing (the previous series) reaches many people at once — building awareness, interest, and demand broadly. Sales engages individual customers directly — having the specific conversations that move a particular customer from interested to purchased. Marketing creates and warms the demand; sales converts specific opportunities.
  • They hand off to each other. In a typical flow, marketing generates awareness and leads (fills the top of the funnel), and sales takes qualified, interested prospects and works with them individually to close (the bottom of the funnel). They’re two halves of getting customers — marketing at scale, sales one-on-one — and they must work together (sales relies on marketing’s demand; marketing relies on sales to convert it).
  • Sales matters most for considered, higher-value purchases. As the GTM series’ “motions” post covered, cheap/simple products can be sold with little or no direct sales (product-led/self-serve), while complex, higher-value, considered purchases need direct sales — a person to understand the customer’s specific situation, address their concerns, and guide the decision. So sales is especially central for enterprise/complex/high-value products (and less so for simple self-serve ones). Knowing when sales is needed (vs self-serve) is itself important.

Sales is the one-to-one complement to marketing’s one-to-many — engaging individual customers to convert them — and it’s most essential for complex, considered, higher-value purchases. With honest sales understood as helping customers make good decisions, the rest of the series covers how it actually works: the process, discovery, the presales/sales-engineer role, demos, objections, and consultative selling.

Why engineers should understand sales

Even if you never become a salesperson, understanding sales is valuable for technical people, for several reasons:

  • Founders must sell. If you start a company, you will have to sell — especially early, when the founder is the salesperson. Founder-led selling (the founder personally selling to early customers) is often how startups get their first customers, and it’s also how founders learn what customers actually want. A technical founder who can’t or won’t sell is severely handicapped. Understanding sales is essential for founders.
  • Engineers increasingly participate in selling. In technical sales (especially B2B), engineers are often directly involved — as sales engineers / solutions engineers (a whole post on this role), in technical evaluations, demos, and proofs of concept. Technical buyers want to talk to technical people, so engineers are frequently part of the sales process whether they sought it or not. Understanding sales makes you effective in these situations.
  • It helps you work with sales and understand the business. Even in a pure engineering role, understanding sales helps you work productively with the sales team (rather than resenting them), understand why the company does what it does (much of which is driven by sales/revenue), and appreciate how your work reaches customers. It’s part of business literacy.
  • Selling skills are broadly useful. The core of sales — understanding others, communicating value, building trust, guiding decisions — is broadly useful: pitching ideas internally, influencing decisions (the EQ series’ influence post), advocating for your work. “Selling” your ideas well is a career skill, and it draws on the same honest-sales fundamentals.

Sales, honestly understood, is the one-to-one work of helping customers understand and decide to adopt a product that solves their problem — a helping discipline built on understanding, not manipulation, and complementary to marketing. The engineer’s disdain targets bad sales; understanding honest sales is essential for founders, common for engineers in technical selling, and broadly useful. Key takeaways

  • The engineer’s disdain for sales targets bad (pushy, manipulative, dishonest) sales — which is real but is neither sales itself nor even effective sales — and generalizing it to all sales is a costly error that leaves your good work unsold, since someone must help customers understand and decide to adopt it.
  • Honest sales is helping a customer understand whether and how your product solves their problem and decide to adopt it — a helping discipline built on understanding the customer (listening, diagnosing) far more than persuasion tricks, and honest by necessity (dishonest selling destroys trust, produces churning customers, and doesn’t last).
  • Sales is the one-to-one complement to marketing’s one-to-many: marketing creates and warms demand broadly, sales converts specific individual opportunities — they hand off (marketing fills the funnel top, sales closes the bottom) and must work together.
  • Sales is most essential for complex, considered, higher-value purchases (which need a person to understand the situation and guide the decision), while cheap/simple products can be sold self-serve/product-led — so knowing when direct sales is needed matters.
  • Understanding sales is valuable even for non-salespeople: founders must sell (especially early, which is also how they learn what customers want), engineers are often directly involved in technical selling (sales/solutions engineering, demos, POCs), it helps you work with sales and understand the business, and the core skills (understanding others, communicating value, building trust) are broadly useful.
