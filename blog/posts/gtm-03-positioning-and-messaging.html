Positioning and Messaging

Positioning is the answer to a question every customer asks in the first five seconds: "what is this, and is it for me?" Get it right and everything else — your website, your sales pitch, your ads — writes itself and lands. Get it wrong and no amount of clever marketing compensates, because you're fluently communicating the wrong thing. Positioning is the most leveraged and most neglected decision in go-to-market, and engineers get it wrong in a predictable way: by describing what they built instead of what it's for.

You know your customer (previous post); now you must define what you are to them and communicate it. This post covers positioning — how you define your product’s place in the customer’s mind relative to alternatives — and messaging — how you express that value so it lands. These are the difference between a product people instantly “get” and one they can’t figure out. For technical people especially, this is where the most value is left on the table.

What positioning is

Positioning is how you define what your product is, who it’s for, and why it’s the right choice — establishing its place in the customer’s mind relative to the alternatives. It’s the context that makes your product make sense. Positioning answers:

  • What category are you in? What kind of thing are you (the frame of reference the customer uses to understand you)? Customers understand new things by comparison to familiar categories — “it’s like X, but for Y.” The category you claim shapes every expectation.
  • Who is it for? Which customer (your ICP) — positioning is always relative to a specific customer, because the same product can be positioned differently for different segments.
  • What’s the key value / differentiation? Why choose you over the alternatives — the one or few things you’re distinctly better at for that customer. Positioning is inherently relative: it defines you against the alternatives (competitors, and the status quo of doing nothing).

Positioning is a choice, and a deliberate one: you decide what to be to whom. The same underlying product can be positioned as a cheap simple tool or a premium powerful platform, as a developer utility or an enterprise solution — and the positioning determines who’s interested, what they expect, what they’ll pay, and who you compete with. It’s upstream of all messaging and marketing: get positioning right and communication is easy; get it wrong and you’re efficiently communicating the wrong thing.

The engineer’s positioning trap

Technical founders and builders make a specific, predictable positioning mistake: describing what the product is (features, how it works) instead of what it does for the customer (the value, the outcome).

  • Features are not value. “We use a distributed event-sourced architecture with sub-millisecond latency” describes the product; it doesn’t tell the customer what problem it solves or why they should care. Customers buy outcomes — time saved, money made, pain removed, a job done — not features or technology. Positioning must lead with the outcome, with features as support for the claim, not the claim itself.
  • You know too much. Having built it, you see the product in terms of its internals and capabilities; the customer sees it in terms of their problem. The curse of knowledge makes builders position around what’s impressive to them rather than what’s valuable to the customer. Bridging that gap — translating capability into customer value — is the core positioning work.
  • The customer’s problem is the anchor. Good positioning starts from the customer’s problem and world (which is why knowing your customer comes first) and frames the product as the solution to that. It answers “why does this matter to me?” before “how does it work?”

Avoiding this trap is the highest-leverage move in technical GTM: stop leading with what you built, and lead with what it does for the customer. This single shift — from feature-first to value-first — fixes the majority of technical products’ positioning and messaging problems.

The value proposition

At the heart of positioning is the value proposition — a clear statement of the value you deliver to your customer: what problem you solve, for whom, and why you’re the better choice. A strong value proposition:

  • Names the customer and their problem. It’s specific about who it’s for and what problem it addresses — grounded in the customer’s actual pain, in their language.
  • States the outcome/benefit. What the customer gets — the concrete improvement to their situation — not a feature list.
  • Explains why you. Why your solution over the alternatives (including doing nothing) — the differentiation that makes you the right choice for that customer.

The value proposition is the compact core of your positioning — the thing that, if a customer understood only one thing, you’d want them to understand. Everything else (website, pitch, ads) elaborates it. A useful test: can a target customer read your value proposition and immediately know what you do, whether it’s for them, and why it’s better? If not, the positioning isn’t clear yet — and no downstream marketing will fix a fuzzy core. Clarifying the value proposition is often the single most valuable GTM exercise a technical team can do.

From positioning to messaging

Messaging is how you express your positioning across all the places customers encounter you — website, sales conversations, ads, product, docs. Positioning is the strategy (what you are and why you matter); messaging is the execution (the words that convey it). Good messaging:

  • Is clear before it’s clever. The goal is to be understood, fast. Clarity — plain language that a target customer instantly gets — beats cleverness, jargon, or vague aspiration. “We do X for Y so they can Z” beats a poetic tagline no one can parse. In GTM, being clearly understood is the whole game.
  • Speaks the customer’s language. Use the words the customer uses for their problem, not your internal or technical vocabulary. Messaging that mirrors how the customer thinks and talks resonates; messaging in your jargon alienates. (This is another reason to talk to customers — you learn their language.)
  • Leads with value, supports with proof. Lead with the outcome/benefit (the value proposition), then support it with evidence — features, proof points, social proof — that make the claim credible. Value first, proof second.
  • Is consistent. The same core message, adapted to each context, everywhere a customer meets you — so the positioning compounds rather than confusing customers with different stories in different places.

Messaging is where positioning meets the customer, and its cardinal rule is clarity: make the value obvious in the customer’s own language, lead with the outcome, and be consistent. A brilliant product with muddled messaging underperforms a clear one; much of GTM success is simply communicating value clearly.

Positioning defines what your product is, who it’s for, and why it’s the right choice relative to alternatives — and its heart is a clear value proposition centered on the customer’s problem and outcome, not your features. Messaging then expresses that positioning in the customer’s language, clearly and consistently. Avoiding the engineer’s feature-first trap — leading with value, not technology — is the highest-leverage move. Next: GTM motions, the fundamental shape of how you sell.

Key takeaways

  • Positioning defines what your product is, who it’s for, and why it’s the right choice relative to alternatives (competitors and the status quo) — it’s a deliberate choice that sets the category/frame customers understand you through, and it’s upstream of all messaging and marketing (right positioning makes communication easy; wrong positioning efficiently communicates the wrong thing).
  • The engineer’s positioning trap is describing what the product is (features, how it works) instead of what it does for the customer (outcome, value) — customers buy outcomes, not technology, so the highest-leverage move is to lead with value and use features only as support.
  • The value proposition is positioning’s compact core: it names the customer and their problem, states the outcome/benefit (not features), and explains why you over the alternatives — test it by whether a target customer instantly knows what you do, if it’s for them, and why it’s better.
  • Messaging expresses positioning wherever customers meet you and its cardinal rule is clarity over cleverness — plain language in the customer’s own vocabulary, leading with value and supported by proof, kept consistent across all touchpoints.
  • Much of GTM success is simply communicating value clearly: a clear product beats a brilliant one with muddled messaging, and clarifying a fuzzy value proposition is often the single most valuable exercise a technical team can do — no downstream marketing fixes a fuzzy core.
