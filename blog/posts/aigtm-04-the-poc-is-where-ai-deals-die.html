The POC Is Where AI Deals Die

The proof-of-concept used to be a formality near the finish line. For AI, it's the crux of the whole deal — and the single most common place AI deals quietly die. Not because the technology failed, but because nobody agreed, up front, what "it worked" would mean. This post is about running an AI POC that converts.

In classic enterprise sales the POC or pilot is a late-stage box to tick. In AI it’s where the buyer’s fear meets reality and the deal is actually decided (why selling AI is different). Get it right and you have an undeniable win; get it wrong and the deal dissolves into “the results were inconclusive.” The difference is almost entirely about what you do before the POC starts.

Why AI POCs die

The failure is rarely technical. AI POCs die of ambiguity:

  • No agreed success criteria. The trial starts with “let’s see how it does.” Without a defined task, bar, and sign-off, there’s no possible outcome that everyone agrees is a win — so even good results read as “promising but not proven,” and the deal stalls indefinitely.
  • The bar moves. With no criteria fixed in advance, every stakeholder applies their own, and the goalposts drift. A result that would have been a clear pass against the original intent fails against a bar that quietly inflated.
  • It runs on the wrong data or task. The POC tests a tidy slice that doesn’t represent the real workload, so a pass doesn’t convince the skeptics — or it tests an unfairly hard case with no baseline, so nothing could pass.
  • No owner, no day-after. Even a technical success stalls if no one owns what happens when it passes. “Great, it works — now what?” with no answer is a dead end.
  • Endless pilot. Without an end condition, the POC becomes a free, perpetual evaluation. The vendor keeps working; the buyer never has to decide.

Notice none of these is “the model wasn’t good enough.” The model is usually fine. The setup killed the deal.

Scope the POC before you run it

The fix is unglamorous and decisive: agree the production success criteria before the trial begins. This is the scope stage of the Deploy-to-Win Loop, and it’s the highest-leverage move in the entire AI sale. Four things, written down, both sides signed:

  • The task — exactly what the system must do, on what inputs, in what workflow. Specific enough that “did it do this?” has a yes/no answer.
  • The bar — the measurable threshold it must clear: accuracy/quality on a representative set, latency, cost per unit. Define it with the customer so it’s theirs, not yours.
  • The data — a representative, real slice of their workload, including the messy and the hard cases — with a baseline (how well the current process or a human does) so the result means something.
  • The day-after — what happens when it passes: who signs off, what the first production step is, what the commercial decision is. A POC with no defined “yes” path is a trap.

Writing this down does something quietly powerful: it converts a hopeful experiment into a mutual decision procedure. When the system clears the agreed bar, the buyer has pre-committed to what that means — and the deal advances on evidence instead of vibes.

Run it like an engineer, sell it like a win

During the POC, evaluate against the bar with real measurement — not a cherry-picked transcript. Show the failure cases honestly and how they’re handled; an anxious buyer trusts a vendor who surfaces problems more than one who hides them. And instrument it so the result is a number the buyer agreed matters, tied to the value quantified in discovery.

A POC run this way produces the technical win — not a feeling that it went well, but documented evidence, against a pre-agreed bar, on real data, that the risk the buyer feared is retired.

Key takeaways

  • For AI, the POC is the crux of the deal, not a late formality — and the most common place AI deals die, almost always from ambiguity, not technical failure.
  • Why AI POCs die: no agreed success criteria, a moving bar, the wrong data/task (unrepresentative or baseline-less), no owner / no day-after, and the endless pilot with no end condition — none of which is “the model wasn’t good enough.”
  • The fix is to scope before running: agree, in writing and signed by both sides, the task (yes/no specific), the bar (measurable, defined with the customer), the data (real, representative, with a baseline), and the day-after (who signs off, first production step, commercial decision).
  • Writing the criteria down converts a hopeful experiment into a decision procedure — clearing the agreed bar means the buyer has pre-committed to what that proves, so the deal advances on evidence.
  • Run it like an engineer, sell it like a win: measure against the bar (not cherry-picked transcripts), surface failure cases honestly (it builds trust), and tie the result to the quantified value from discovery — producing a documented technical win, not a good feeling.
