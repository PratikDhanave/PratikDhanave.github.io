Semantic and Hybrid Search — Matching by Meaning

Keyword search has one incurable weakness: it matches words, not meaning, so it misses relevant results that use different vocabulary than the query. Semantic search fixes this by representing queries and documents as vectors of meaning and matching by proximity in that space — the technology behind modern RAG. But the surprising lesson of production search is that semantic didn't replace keyword; the best systems combine them. This post explains vector search and why hybrid retrieval wins.

Keyword search matches words, not meaning. Semantic search embeds queries and documents into a vector space and matches by proximity — the engine of RAG — but it's weak at exact matches. Why keyword and semantic have opposite strengths, and why hybrid retrieval (not replacement) is the modern default.

Ranking — TF-IDF and BM25, the Math of Relevance

Finding the documents that contain your words is the easy part; putting the best one first is where search lives or dies. For decades, the answer to "how relevant is this document to this query?" has come from two intuitions made precise: a word matters more in a document that uses it often, and matters less if it's common across all documents. TF-IDF and BM25 turn those intuitions into the scoring functions that rank most of the world's text search — and understanding them demystifies why search returns what it does.

Finding matches is easy; putting the best first is where search lives. Two intuitions — a word matters more when frequent in a document, less when common across all — become TF-IDF and then BM25, the decades-long default (and the keyword score in hybrid RAG). Its lexical ceiling is the vocabulary gap.
