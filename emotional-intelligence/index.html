Emotional Intelligence

Software is built by people, so the hardest problems are rarely purely technical. Emotional intelligence — reading yourself and others clearly and acting on that awareness — is what makes collaboration, code review, incident response, and mentoring actually work. This page lays out the framework I lean on as an engineer and mentor, plus a curated set of public resources to go deeper.

These are principles and well-established models, framed for an engineering context — not a personal narrative. Use them as a starting point for your own practice.

The framework I use

The model I return to most is the five-domain view of emotional intelligence popularized by psychologist and science journalist Daniel Goleman. It divides EI into two inward-facing competencies (self-awareness and self-regulation), one that drives you (motivation), and two outward-facing ones (empathy and social skills). Below is each domain in my own words, paired with a concrete, generic way it shows up in engineering work.

Self-Awareness

Noticing your own emotions, strengths, limits, and triggers as they happen — and understanding how they shape your decisions. It is the foundation the other four domains rest on, because you cannot manage what you cannot see.

In engineering: Recognizing when you are attached to a design because you wrote it, or when frustration in a stalled debugging session is pushing you toward a rushed fix. Naming that state to yourself is what lets you slow down and choose a better response.

#Inward #Foundation

Self-Regulation

Managing your impulses and reactions so you respond deliberately instead of reflexively. It is not about suppressing feelings — it is about creating a gap between the feeling and the action, and using that gap well.

In engineering: Staying calm and methodical during an incident or on-call page, when adrenaline pushes everyone toward blame or panic. A regulated responder keeps the focus on mitigation first, root cause second, and postmortem without finger-pointing after.

#Inward #Incident Response

Motivation

An internal drive to do good work for reasons beyond external reward — curiosity, craft, and a sense of purpose. Intrinsically motivated people stay optimistic and persistent when a problem gets hard or a result is disappointing.

In engineering: Caring that a system is correct and maintainable even when no one is watching the internals — writing the test, the doc, or the clear error message because it is the right thing, not because a reviewer demanded it. This is the energy that sustains long refactors and unglamorous reliability work.

#Drive #Craft

Empathy

Understanding what other people are experiencing and considering their perspective before you act. It means reading the unstated — the concern behind a question, the pressure behind a deadline — not just the literal words.

In engineering: Empathy in code review changes everything. Reviewing the change on its merits, assuming the author had good reasons, and phrasing feedback so it teaches rather than scolds turns review from a gate into a conversation. The same skill helps you write APIs and error messages for the human who will use them later.

#Outward #Code Review

Social Skills

Building rapport, communicating clearly, and moving groups toward a shared goal. It is the applied surface of the other four domains — how self-awareness and empathy show up in how you actually work with people.

In engineering: Running a cross-team design discussion where several strong opinions have to converge, surfacing disagreement early, and helping the group commit to a decision everyone can support. Good social skills are what let a distributed team stay aligned without heavyweight process.

#Outward #Collaboration

Practices

Frameworks only matter if they turn into habits. These are practical, everyday practices that make the five domains concrete — small enough to actually use in a standup, a review, or a heated thread.

Habits that build EI on a team

  • Listen actively. Let people finish, then reflect back what you heard before responding. Most conflict is really a misread, and reflecting first catches it early.
  • Assume positive intent. Start from the belief that the other person is competent and acting in good faith. It changes the tone of every disagreement and is almost always the more accurate assumption.
  • Separate your ego from your code. A critique of a design is not a critique of you. Holding your work loosely lets you accept better ideas without feeling diminished.
  • Frame feedback with care. Be specific, focus on the work rather than the person, and pair a concern with a path forward. Feedback that lands is feedback that gets acted on.
  • Pause before you react. When something lands wrong, wait before replying — a few minutes, or a night for anything sensitive. The gap between feeling and action is where good judgment lives.

Resources

A curated shortlist of well-known public resources on emotional intelligence and the communication skills around it. These are the sources I point people to; start anywhere the description resonates.

"Emotional Intelligence" — Daniel Goleman

The 1995 book that brought the term into the mainstream and popularized the five-domain model this page is built on. The best starting point for the framework itself.

#Foundational #Framework
Overview

Salovey & Mayer — the academic origin

Psychologists Peter Salovey and John D. Mayer coined "emotional intelligence" in their 1990 academic work and developed the ability-based model that predates the popular one. Worth reading for the rigorous roots of the concept.

#Academic #Origins
Overview

"Nonviolent Communication" — Marshall B. Rosenberg

A practical method for expressing needs and hearing others without blame or judgment. Directly useful for the empathy and social-skills domains, especially in tense conversations.

#Communication #Empathy
Overview

"Crucial Conversations" — Patterson, Grenny, McMillan & Switzler

A field guide to high-stakes conversations where opinions differ and emotions run strong. Strong on self-regulation and staying constructive when it matters most.

#Difficult Conversations #Self-Regulation
Overview

"Radical Candor" — Kim Scott

A model for giving feedback that is both caring and direct, written for managers and mentors. A natural bridge from EI theory to how you actually run reviews and one-on-ones.

#Feedback #Leadership
Overview

"Working with Emotional Intelligence" — Daniel Goleman

Goleman's follow-up applying the framework specifically to the workplace and professional performance. A good next read once the core model clicks.

#Workplace #Applied

Why this sits next to the technical work

The best engineering cultures I have been part of were not the ones with the cleverest code — they were the ones where people felt safe to disagree, ask questions, and be wrong. Emotional intelligence is how you build that, and it is inseparable from how I approach mentoring and leadership.
